A buyer is a contractor or a service pro moving between jobs. When a new lead lands they want to know, and the thing in their hand is a phone, not a dashboard. The lead that sits unseen for ten minutes is the lead that doesn't close.

So the job is to get a lead from the agency's CRM to the right buyer's phone. This post covers the version that shipped at LeadSwitchboard. It's simpler than what I'd have drawn on a whiteboard, and the most useful lesson in it came from an outage.

## The path

A CRM like Go High Level (GHL) posts the lead to a generic webhook with a campaign slug. Meta lead ads have their own endpoint that verifies Meta's signature, pulls the full lead from the Graph API, and hands off to the same ingestion code. Either way, the platform does the rest inside that request:

```
CRM
  → POST /leads/webhook/generic   (API key with leads:write, campaign slug)
  → upsert lead on (agency_id, source, external_id)
  → distribute inline: pool, filter, rank, lock credits, assign
  → commit
  → after_commit: SMS, email, and push to each assigned buyer
  → 201 { distribution, assigned_count, assigned_buyer_ids }
```

There's no queue between the webhook and the buyer. The [distribution engine](/blog/lead-distribution-engine) runs in the request, and by the time the sender gets its response, the buyer's phone has already buzzed.

## Why one request

The platform takes about 3.5 leads a day. The busiest hour in a month saw three. A queue at that volume adds a place for leads to sit and a worker to keep alive, and buys nothing.

The platform does have a Redis worker. It handles credit-balance emails, auto-recharge results, and dispute digests, and it runs as a Lambda on a 15-minute schedule. That's fine for a low-balance email. It's the wrong home for a lead, where 15 minutes is the difference between a call and a voicemail.

Running inline also puts the result in the response. The sender gets back `assigned_count` and `assigned_buyer_ids`, so whoever wired up the integration can see in their own logs which buyers got the lead.

The trade-off is that the request is only as fast as its slowest step. If the SMS provider is slow, the webhook response is slow. At this volume I'll take that over a second moving part.

## Notifications wait for the commit

Distribution doesn't send anything while it runs. It appends what it wants to send to `session.info`, and a SQLAlchemy `after_commit` listener picks it up:

```python
@event.listens_for(Session, "after_commit")
def handle_lead_assignment_after_commit(session: Session) -> None:
    items = session.info.pop("post_commit_lead_notifications", None)
    if not items:
        return

    with fresh_session_with_retry() as db:
        for item in items:
            try:
                _send_notifications_for_assignment(
                    db, item["lead_id"], item["buyer_id"], item["agency_id"]
                )
                db.commit()
            except Exception as e:
                logger.error(f"Error processing lead assignment notification item: {e}", exc_info=True)
                db.rollback()
```

Two properties fall out of this. A buyer never gets a text for an assignment that rolled back, because the listener only fires on a durable commit. And a failed text never undoes an assignment, because notifications run on their own session, one item at a time. The same buffer-then-commit pattern carries the agency's Slack alerts, Zapier hooks, and buyer webhooks.

## Duplicates

CRMs resend. The webhook dedupes on the lead itself, keyed on `(agency_id, source, external_id)`. A repeat doesn't get rejected. It updates the existing lead and runs distribution again.

A second check catches the same person arriving under a new ID. Inside distribution, any buyer who already received that phone number or email in the last 30 days is skipped with `duplicate_contact`.

Meta's webhook gets one more layer. Each delivery writes a `WebhookEvent` row keyed on the leadgen ID, with a unique constraint, so a redelivered batch is a no-op.

GHL used to have its own webhook with its own event table. I retired it in October 2026. It routed leads by service line alone and never ran the campaign mapping that every other source goes through. GHL accounts now post to the generic webhook with a campaign slug, and the old route returns 410 with a message pointing there.

## The dashboard is the second channel

The buyer's pipeline page fetches its board with a single SWR call and no options:

```ts
const { data: boardData, mutate: mutateBoard } =
  useSWR<DashboardResponse>(boardUrl, dashboardFetcher);
```

There's no polling and no push. SWR's defaults refetch when the page mounts and when the tab regains focus, and that's when it matters. The buyer gets the text, opens the dashboard, and sees the lead. A live-updating board would only help someone staring at it, and nobody stares at a dashboard waiting for a lead.

## The outage

On August 29, 2026, distribution started failing. It ran that way for 3.5 days before anyone noticed.

The webhook did what it was written to do. It caught the exception, kept the lead, and returned success. Meta saw a 200. The lead looked normal in the database. The error went to CloudWatch, and nothing read CloudWatch.

The fix (#676) was a metric filter on the `Error distributing lead` log line, an alarm on that metric, and an SNS topic that emails two people. I checked it against 30 days of logs: 18 errors across the four outage days, none on the other 26. It would have paged in five minutes instead of 3.5 days.

It pages by email, not Slack. The Slack bot was failing with `not_in_channel`, and an alarm that can't deliver isn't an alarm.

I also removed an alarm. There had been one on the rate of leads assigned to nobody. Measured against the same 30 days, its floor of five leads an hour was met in 0 of 91 active hours, so it could never have fired. And the normal zero-assigned rate is 60%, since plenty of leads legitimately match no buyer. No rate threshold separates broken from normal at that volume. The config comment now says to reinstate it "only with numbers, not intuition."

## Why the webhook still returns 200 on failure

The obvious reaction to that outage is to return a 500, so the sender retries. I kept the 200 on purpose.

Meta only retries for hours, and the outage ran for days. By the time a retry arrived, the `WebhookEvent` row was already marked terminal, so the retry would dedupe to a no-op anyway. A retry can't recover a multi-day failure. A person can. The comment in the handler says it plainly: "The error alarm is the recovery path, not the retry."

When distribution fails now, the failure shows up in three places. The agency's Slack gets a `lead.distribution_failed` alert. The admin leads page shows a banner counting failed leads, leaving out the expected ones like no-match and plan caps. And the lead's [distribution trace](/blog/distribution-trace) shows which step it died on.

## What I measure

The speed-to-lead number in analytics is `viewed_at − delivered_at`: how long a buyer takes to open a lead after it reaches them. That's the number buyers can change.

I don't time the pipeline itself. `Lead.distribution_started_at` exists as a column, and nothing writes it. With every step in one request, the request duration is close enough for now. If the path ever grows a queue, that column is the first thing I'll fill in.

## What this teaches

**Build for the volume you have.** A live-push dashboard over Redis pub/sub is the right design for a lot of products. At 3.5 leads a day, the phone is the real-time channel, and the dashboard only needs to be right when someone opens it.

**A success response can hide a dead pipeline.** The webhook was correct to return 200 and wrong to be the only signal. Errors you catch on purpose still need something that reads them.

**Measure before you alarm.** One alarm looked reasonable and could never fire. The other looked crude and would have caught the outage in five minutes. I only knew which was which after counting.
